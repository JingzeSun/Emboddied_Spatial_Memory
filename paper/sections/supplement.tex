\section{Zero-training LLM operator}
\label{app:llm}
We asked whether a frozen LLM, given the same sealed feature tables as text, can choose the operations without training.
The model was DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (API alias \texttt{deepseek-flash}, accessed 2026-10-06, thinking enabled, other parameters at the provider's defaults).
Each frame takes up to two calls: one table of recalled pairs and birth options, answered by one choice per fragment and turned into costs for the same solver; then, after the solve, one table of judgeable entities, answered by \op{RETRACT} or \op{NOOP} for each (skipped in frames without judgeable entities).
The instructions contain only the task, the column definitions and a synthetic format example.
A frame took about 16k reasoning tokens with instance masks and 37k with SAM~2.1 masks; this episode cost 19.26 USD in total over both front ends (plus a 6.08 USD pilot on 200 frames of a training episode), and the originally registered list of 15 episodes per front end was estimated at about 250 USD off-peak to 500 USD at peak, the longest episode alone taking several days. We therefore ran one validation episode per front end, fixed before any call by a registered draw (ascending SHA-256 of a salt and the episode identifier; the original list of 15 candidates was written before the run and is kept in the repository).
The episode (430 frames) has no identity, retrieval, residual or recovery events, so only node metrics are compared.
Invalid answers fell back to a default for at most 0.93\% of calls.
On this episode the operator reached node F1 0.696 with instance masks and 0.434 with SAM~2.1 masks (F1$_\mathrm{IoU}$ 0.376 and 0.209), and almost all of its retractions were wrong (false-retract rate 0.976 and 0.982).
Table~\ref{tab:llm_op} places these values next to every other arm on the same episode, each with its own validation mean and the percentile of this episode in its validation distribution.
The episode is not unusually long or crowded: 430 frames (24th percentile of the 43 validation episodes) and 9.2 and 10.6 fragments per frame (43rd and 64th percentile).
The other arms' node F1 on it lies between the 20th and 83rd percentile of their own validation distributions with instance masks, and between the 29th and 73rd with SAM~2.1 masks except \arm{LOW} (8th).
The retracting rule arms and the \arm{HandCost} and \arm{HeuristicLabel} ablations also retracted almost only objects that were still in place on this episode (false-retract rate 0.985--1.000 on both front ends); VSMT-lean's was 0.943 and \arm{NoVersion}'s 0.893 with SAM~2.1 masks (96th and 91st percentile of their validation distributions); both are undefined with instance masks because at least one seed made no judged retraction there.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}
Every table in this paper is generated by \path{paper/tools/make_tables.py} and every data figure by \path{paper/tools/make_figures.py} from committed exports; the paper index (\path{results/vsmt_lean_s3_06_paper_index_154043e.json}) maps each table of the main study to its file, field, command and commit, and the LLM-op context comes from \path{results/vsmt_lean_s4_llm_op_context_2b50a12.json} (\path{ops/vsmt/s4_llm_op_context.py}).
The statistics can be recomputed from the committed merged audits with the frozen code, and a read-only re-run reproduced them value for value (\path{ops/vsmt/s3_06_reanalysis.py}).
Learned weights are bit-identical for the same commit and thread count on the hardware we verified; audits are deterministic; the generator reproduces episode structure, not bytes; LLM answers are archived and replayed byte for byte, while the API itself is not reproducible.
